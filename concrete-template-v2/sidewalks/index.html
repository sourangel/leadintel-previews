What's included.

Walkways fail at the edges and the joints: a base that settles, a slope that holds water, or tree roots nobody planned around. We lay out the line, grade for drainage and place joints on a plan. Public sidewalks and anything that ties into the street usually need a permit, and we tell you up front.

  • Straight and curved front walks and garden paths
  • Sidewalk replacement where the old one has heaved or cracked
  • Grading so water drains away from the house and the walk
  • Steps and landings that tie into the walkway
  • Broom, smooth, colored or stamped finishes
  • Joints planned for the layout and for nearby tree roots

How we do it.

  1. 1

    Mark the line

    We walk the route with you, mark the layout and check slope and drainage.

  2. 2

    Prep and form

    Excavate, compact the base and set forms, curved or straight, to the line.

  3. 3

    Pour and finish

    Place and finish with a grip surface, then tool the joints where they belong.

  4. 4

    Cure and protect

    Keep it from drying too fast and keep traffic off until it is ready.

Signs your walkway needs work.

If any of these sound familiar, the sooner someone looks at it the cheaper it usually is to fix.

  • Sections that have lifted into a trip edge
  • Standing water or ice patches that never clear
  • Cracks that have moved or opened
  • A worn surface that is slippery when wet

Recent work.

Sidewalks questions.

Can you pour a curved walkway?

Yes. We form curves with flexible forms and plan the joints so they follow the shape instead of fighting it.

Do I need a permit for a sidewalk?

Walkways on your own property usually do not. Anything in the public right of way, or tying into the street or a curb, usually does. We tell you what applies to yours.

How soon can I walk on it?

Most walkways take foot traffic after a day or two. We tell you when yours is ready.
